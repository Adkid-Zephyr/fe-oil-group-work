Abstract

This study asks whether a small, interpretable information set improves one-month-ahead forecasts of the supplied front-month crude-oil futures series. An ordinary least squares model uses recent oil-price changes, the front-versus-second-month price spread and a conservatively lagged dollar return. It is evaluated through 114 expanding-window forecasts from January 2015 to June 2024. Its root mean squared error is 12.92 percentage points, compared with 13.41 for the expanding historical mean and 13.44 for a zero-change forecast. The improvement is modest: a paired block-bootstrap interval for the difference in squared errors includes zero. The model provides a transparent way to organise information, but these results do not establish a reliable trading advantage.

Introduction

An oil trader needs a forecast that can be understood, challenged and compared with a realistic alternative. A complicated forecasting system is difficult to defend if its apparent accuracy comes from using future information or fitting past noise. Our question is therefore deliberately narrow: can three economically interpretable signals improve next-month movements in the supplied CL1 series compared with simple forecasts?

The objective is to assess incremental forecasting value, not to explain every movement in oil prices. We choose a monthly horizon because the supplied macroeconomic variables are monthly and a monthly decision cycle is easy to communicate. The cost is a small sample and the loss of within-month detail. A monthly model is not a tool for intraday execution or for managing a rapidly changing crisis. All conclusions refer to this historical workbook, which ends in June 2024 for the monthly sheets, rather than to a current market forecast.

Data and target

The source is the course workbook, Oil Data.xlsx. We use CL1 and CL2 from Industry_M and DXY returns from Macro_M. There are 355 monthly industry observations between December 1994 and June 2024. Rows are sorted chronologically and joined by calendar month: industry dates can be the last business day while macro dates are calendar month-end. A complete monthly grid is checked before constructing lags. We do not fill missing values, winsorise extremes or remove the pandemic. Lag construction leaves 353 complete observations.

The target is the monthly percentage change, rt = 100(Pt/Pt−1 − 1). Changes are more meaningful for a directional trading discussion than the dollar price level, and the initial-sample unit-root tests support this choice. Importantly, CL1 is a continuous front-month series. The workbook does not document contract identifiers, rolling rules or adjustment conventions. Its percentage change must therefore be described as a price-change proxy, not as the realised return on a continuously held futures position. Monthly prices in this sheet are positive; that does not mean daily oil prices were always positive.

Our first signal is the preceding month's CL1 change. It asks whether recent movement tends to persist or reverse. The second is 100(CL1 − CL2)/CL1 at the preceding month-end. A positive value indicates that the nearby contract is more expensive than the next contract. This curve-shape measure may contain information about near-term scarcity or expectations, but its regression coefficient is an empirical association, not a causal supply estimate or a guaranteed carry return.

The third signal is the DXY return, multiplied by 100 to convert the workbook's decimal return to percentage points. Dollar movements may be associated with demand conditions and dollar-denominated commodity prices. We use the observation from two months before the target month, imposing an extra month of delay because publication timestamps are not documented. This reduces potential timing ambiguity but may sacrifice useful information. Inventories, production and other macroeconomic levels are excluded to keep the model explainable and avoid adding uncertain release lags and revision histories. That choice does not establish that those variables lack predictive value.

Methodology

The model is rt = α + β1rt−1 + β2ct−1 + β3dt−2 + εt. It has one intercept and three slopes. This compact OLS specification makes every prediction decomposable into a base forecast and three contributions. We use no feature search, nonlinear model, interaction term or tuned hyperparameter. The specification and evaluation cut-off were recorded before computing the forecast results in this exercise; this is an internal protocol, not an externally registered study.

The initial estimation sample runs from February 1995 to December 2014 and contains 239 complete observations. Evaluation begins in January 2015. At each target month, the model is refitted using complete observations with target dates strictly earlier than that month. The resulting forecast is stored before moving to the next observation. This expanding-window procedure imitates sequential learning and avoids a random train-test split. The workbook itself is a historical snapshot rather than a vintage database, so correctly ordered code cannot prove that every input was available in exactly that form in real time.

Two benchmarks make the comparison transparent. The zero-change benchmark predicts no movement; the expanding historical mean predicts the average of past target changes available to the model. The latter is the designated primary benchmark. We report RMSE, which penalises large mistakes, and MAE, which is less dominated by extremes. Directional accuracy counts correct positive or negative forecasts; a zero forecast has no directional call. Out-of-sample R-squared is one minus the model's squared-error sum divided by the historical-mean benchmark's squared-error sum. It is not the usual in-sample regression R-squared.

Results

Across 114 evaluation months, OLS produces RMSE of 12.917 and MAE of 8.498 percentage points. The historical-mean benchmark gives 13.408 and 8.672 respectively, while zero change gives 13.438 and 8.739. Relative to the historical mean, the model reduces RMSE by about 3.66% and squared forecast error by 7.18%. Its direction accuracy is 57.89%, compared with 56.14% for the historical mean. These are small gains in a noisy prediction problem, not evidence of consistently accurate forecasts.

The stress-period breakdown is informative. For 2015–2019, model RMSE is 8.89 against 9.16 for the historical mean. During 2020, it rises to 30.55 against 32.01. For January 2021–June 2024, the figures are 8.56 and 8.68. All three descriptive subsets favour the model on this metric, but the gains are limited and the crisis errors are very large. These subsets are reported for explanation, not used to select a different model or to exclude difficult observations.

To illustrate sampling uncertainty, we resample paired monthly squared-error differences using a circular moving-block bootstrap, with six-month blocks, 2,000 draws and a fixed random seed. The mean difference, OLS minus historical mean, is −12.91 squared percentage points. The approximate 95% percentile interval is [−34.54, 1.11]. Because it includes zero, this exercise does not establish a robust improvement at that interval level. The calculation resamples realised forecast losses rather than re-estimating models inside each bootstrap sample. Its validity also depends on how well these historical blocks represent forecast-loss dependence, particularly across crises.

Diagnostics and interpretation

Diagnostics are computed on the initial training sample so that they do not silently redesign the model after observing the evaluation results. The ADF test with an intercept and AIC-selected lags fails to reject a unit root in CL1 levels (p = 0.340), but rejects it for the monthly target changes (p < 0.001). The three regressors also reject the unit-root null in this sample. These tests support using transformed variables, but do not prove a permanently stable data-generating process.

The residual Ljung–Box test through lag 12 gives p = 0.092: there is no rejection at 5%, although this is not proof of independence. The Koenker version of the Breusch–Pagan test rejects constant variance (p = 0.011). We therefore report HAC standard errors with three lags for coefficient interpretation. HAC adjusts inference; it does not fix forecast errors, eliminate heteroskedasticity or create reliable prediction intervals. The OLS-residual CUSUM test gives p = 0.244, offering no rejection of stability in the initial sample, with limited power against some forms of change.

The initial slopes are approximately 0.120 for momentum, −0.545 for the curve-shape measure and −0.226 for the lagged dollar return. Only the curve-shape coefficient excludes zero in its reported 95% HAC interval. Its negative sign should be explained honestly rather than forced into a standard scarcity story: in this sample, a higher nearby premium is associated with lower subsequent CL1 percentage changes after conditioning on the other variables. Confounding, mean reversion and construction of the continuous series may contribute. In-sample R-squared is only 2.51%, and individual significance across several tests is not a guarantee of predictive usefulness.

Implications for a trading desk

The useful output is a transparent forecast discussion. For any evaluation month, the interactive page shows the information dates, fitted coefficients, each signal's contribution and the observed outcome. A trader can identify whether the forecast is driven by recent returns, curve shape or the dollar instead of treating it as a black box. A scenario control changes inputs while holding that month's fitted coefficients fixed; it is a sensitivity illustration, not a newly validated forecast.

A simple illustrative rule goes long when the forecast exceeds +1 percentage point, short below −1 and remains flat otherwise. The threshold is chosen for explanation, not optimised. The page allows users to see how thresholds and assumed costs alter activity and a price-change-based score. Costs are deducted per unit of absolute position change, including entry and final exit. This score is an arithmetic sum of signed proxy changes. It is not a wealth curve or executable futures profit: actual contracts, margin, funding, rolling, slippage and trading-time prices are absent. A credible trading backtest requires those inputs before any profitability claim.

Conclusion

A three-signal linear model offers a modest historical forecast improvement and a clear explanation of how each estimate is produced. The evidence supports presenting it as a disciplined analytical starting point, while retaining simple benchmarks and highlighting its large stress-period errors. The next useful step is to verify data construction and timing, then test the unchanged model on genuinely unseen observations. Adding complexity solely to obtain a more impressive backtest would weaken the clarity of the current exercise.
